Why Won't My Wood Stove's Secondary Burn Light?

If you only ever see one flame at the wood and never the dancing second flame above it, something on this list is the reason - and the fix is almost never the stove itself.

What secondary burn actually needs

Wood does not burn as a solid. Heat drives off flammable gases starting around 500°F, and those gases need a second, hotter zone - roughly 1100°F - plus enough fresh air to actually catch fire. Miss either one and that smoke goes up the flue unburned instead of adding a second flame and more heat to your room.

Why it is not lighting

University of Missouri Extension, G1731 "Wood Stove Operation" names the same three root causes for any incomplete burn: insufficient air supply, misapplied air, or insufficient heat. In practice, that shows up as one of these:

What you seeWhat is actually wrong
Fire smoulders, hisses, hard to keep litWood is too wet. Fresh-cut wood is roughly half water by weight; see how long your species needs to season before it can burn hot enough for a second flame at all.
Never happens right after loadingA cold stove has not reached ignition temperature yet. Build a hot, briskly burning fire first on kindling and small splits, and only add the full load once that is established.
Dies down as soon as you damp the air controlAir was misapplied, not just reduced - closing the intake too far starves the exact air the second chamber needs, even while the first chamber still has flame.
Weak draft, smoke backs up when you open the doorThe chimney has not warmed up to pull air yet, or a cold flue is choking supply. Warming the flue briefly before lighting the main load helps establish draft.
Getting worse over the season, more smoke each weekCreosote is building up. University of Missouri Extension, G1731 "Wood Stove Operation" lists its causes as wet wood, incomplete combustion, cool surfaces - each of which is also, separately, a reason secondary burn will not light. A flue that drops below about 250°F is cool enough for creosote to condense, which then further narrows the draft and makes the next fire worse.

The idea is older than your stove

Splitting combustion into two stages - air below the fire for the main burn, air above it for a second chamber that burns what the first one missed - is not a recent invention. US4320738A ("Heating stove"), filed 1980-05-30 and granted to Virgil Johnson on 1982-03-23, describes exactly that: A channel splits incoming air so some goes in below the fuel and some above it, feeding two separate combustion chambers instead of one. A control rod links a baffle and a damper to route the exhaust through either a straight flue or a longer serpentine one. Splitting air below the fire from air above it, into a second combustion stage, is the same basic idea sold today as "secondary burn" or "secondary air tubes" on EPA-certified stoves - this patent is one disclosed, dated record of that idea being worked out and written down decades before it became a marketing term.

This stove invention relates to wood and coal burning stoves employed for heating. More effective draft control and heat transfer is achieved by a stove employing straight and serpentine flues, a control rod to coordinate movement of a baffle and damper for defining passageways to the flues, and a channel for apportioning air above and below the fuel and into first and second combustion chambers.

Patent status: Expired - Lifetime. Filed before 1995, so US law gives it the LONGER of 17 years from grant (1999-03-23) or 20 years from filing (2000-05-30) - term ran out 2000-05-30, over 25 years ago. This patent's own claims stopped constraining anybody in 2000. That is not a clearance opinion on any specific stove sold today - a modern stove could still read on some other, later, still-live patent.
