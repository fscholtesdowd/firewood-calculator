How to Use a Firewood Moisture Meter

A meter takes ten seconds and ends the guessing that a stack "feels dry" - and most wrong readings come from testing the wrong spot, not a bad meter.

What the number needs to be

U.S. EPA Burn Wise, "Test Your Wood with a Moisture Meter": wood measuring 15%–20% moisture content burns cleanest, and anything under 20% counts as dry and ready to burn. That matches what this site already says about seasoning - freshly cut wood is roughly half water by weight, and a stove spends its first minutes boiling that off before any heat reaches the room.

How to get a reading you can trust

U.S. EPA Burn Wise, "Test Your Wood with a Moisture Meter" lays out the test in three steps:

  1. Split the wood
  2. Test the newly split face
  3. Insert the probes into the wood grain

The reason for step 2 is the one most people skip: a log's outer surface and cut ends dry out first no matter how wet the core still is, so a meter pressed against bark or an end grain reads low even on wood that will still hiss and smoulder. Split the piece and test the freshly exposed face instead - that is the number that actually predicts how it will burn. Check more than one piece from the stack; one dry-feeling log at the top does not mean the pile is seasoned all the way through.

What kind of meter to look for

Most meters sold for firewood are the pin type: two short metal probes on the end that you press into the wood. It reads the electrical resistance between the two pins as a moisture percentage - wetter wood conducts better, so the reading climbs. That is also why the probes have to go into the freshly split face and not just rest on the surface: resistance between the pins only means something once they are actually in contact with the wood, not bark. Look for one that covers at least 0–40%, since green firewood can start well above the 20% dry line before it has seasoned at all.

No meter handy?

The no-tool tells on the seasoning-time page still work: dry wood is noticeably lighter, its ends have checked and cracked, and two pieces knocked together ring instead of thud. A meter just turns those tells into a number, and catches wood that looks dry on the outside but is not.

Source: U.S. EPA Burn Wise, "Test Your Wood with a Moisture Meter" (EPA-456/F-20-003).
